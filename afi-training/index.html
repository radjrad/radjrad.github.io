      Why Afi guards

      Resource guarding isn't dominance and it isn't "bad behavior" — it's anxiety about losing something valuable. When Afi hovers over his ball, freezes at his bowl, or explodes at the sound of the downstairs dog, the driving emotion is fear of loss. That's why this program never takes things from him and never punishes the warning signs. Instead it changes the math: another dog appearing near my stuff = great things happen to me.

      The three methods this program uses

      • Counter-conditioning & desensitization (CC/DS) — pair the trigger (a dog appearing, someone approaching the bowl) at a low intensity with high-value food until his emotional response flips from "threat" to "jackpot predictor". Intensity only rises as fast as his body stays loose.
      • Trade-up game — "drop" is never a loss. He gives up the ball, gets something as good or better, and usually gets the ball straight back. Over hundreds of reps, letting go stops being scary.
      • Engage–disengage ("Look at That") — he learns that calmly looking at a dog and then looking back at you is a rewardable skill. This replaces the stare→stiffen→explode chain with look→check in→treat.

      Reading Afi's body

      • Green (keep going): loose wiggly body, soft mouth, takes treats gently, can look away from the trigger.
      • Yellow (add distance NOW): freezing, hovering over the item, hard stare, closed mouth, slow-motion movement, grabbing treats hard or refusing them.
      • Red (session over, leave calmly): growl escalating, snap, lunge. Don't scold — just create distance and make the next session easier.

      Management (until training catches up)

      • At the park: bring two identical balls; put the ball away when other dogs approach; skip fetch on crowded days.
      • Water: give Afi his own bowl away from other dogs; the walker should pick the bowl up between dogs, and never let strange dogs approach while he drinks.
      • Tell the walker: no shared resources with other dogs yet, keep 10m+ from other dogs at rest stops, and reward calm looking at dogs.
      • At home: when the downstairs dog is active, preempt with a scatter-feed or stuffed Kong on his mat before he winds up.

      When to bring in a professional

      This program is safe DIY groundwork. Bring in a certified force-free professional (look for CPDT-KA, KPA-CTP, IAABC, or a veterinary behaviorist) if: a bite breaks skin, incidents get worse over 3–4 weeks despite training, or you want a spotter for the dog-near-resource levels. Guarding against dogs is harder to fix alone than guarding against humans because you can't control the other dog's behavior.
